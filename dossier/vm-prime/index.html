ETHICBEHAVIOR ENGINEERING COMPANYRe-engineering the way people use things.

Discover friction · Engineer behavior · Scale useful change

ETHIC was born from a simple observation: people get used to unnecessary effort. They repeat the same workaround, accept the same awkward step, or learn to live with a product that does not quite fit the task. ETHIC looks for those normalised but unresolved frictions—then investigates whether a practical physical or technology-enabled intervention can change the way the task gets done.

It is not about inventing another gadget because a category is popular. It is about identifying the remaining friction inside a familiar workflow, checking what existing solutions have already fixed, and rebuilding only where a meaningful gap survives.

00 / The premise

Friction is the signal.
Normalisation is the blind spot.

In ETHIC's framework, friction is repeated extra effort, an avoidable error, a missed action, a clumsy hand-off, awkward reach, inconsistent output, repeated setup or cleanup—or a workaround that users have accepted as “just how it works”. The opportunity starts by seeing this pattern clearly, not by jumping to a product sketch.

01

Observe the actual behaviour

What does the person do before, during and after the task? Where do they pause, grip, reach, repeat, switch tools or improvise?

02

Find the unofficial interface

Look for tape, clips, bands, props, repeated reminders, unusual positioning or small rituals people create to make products cooperate.

03

Test the residual gap

Existing products may already solve the issue. ETHIC needs the specific failure that remains—across users and conditions—not merely a new-looking design.

ETHIC's governing rule: identify the friction people have stopped noticing; isolate its mechanism; verify that the gap still exists after checking available products and prior art; then engineer a change that is simple to understand, repeatable to use and capable of scaling across contexts.
01 / F1–F4

Micro-frictions.
Small interventions can change the task.

These are reference cases used to teach the discovery lens—not four new ETHIC products. Each case begins with a task people already perform, identifies the source of friction, then explains how a product or format change altered the workflow. The evidence also shows why history and product precedent must be checked before claiming novelty.

Micro case 01 · Format transitionBlue / Oral care

Tooth powder → toothpaste tube

The product category evolved from loose powder to paste in a jar and then to a collapsible tube. The key lens is the use experience—not an unsupported claim that one annoyance invented toothpaste.

1850sModern toothpaste in jars appears
1873Colgate mass-produces jar toothpaste
1890sColgate sells collapsible tubes
Observed friction: loose powder requires opening, tipping or scooping; the user must manage a dry product and its container every time. A paste and tube make the amount easier to dispense directly, keep the product contained between uses, and simplify storage and travel.
  • What changed: the routine moved from “open → shake/scoop → close” toward “open cap → squeeze → close”.
  • What it solved: less handling and a more convenient, contained dispensing workflow. It did not make all oral-care problems disappear.
  • Market lesson: Smithsonian records Colgate jar toothpaste in the 1870s and collapsible tubes in the 1890s. The museum also notes powder continued into the 20th century and that toothpaste had overtaken tooth powder in the US by 1949. This was a gradual, multi-factor transition—not a single global launch date.

Micro case 02 · Retention / routingRed / Cable management

Cable organisers

As desks and devices accumulate power and data leads, the cable itself becomes a small but recurring workflow problem: it moves, tangles, drops out of reach and makes the work surface harder to manage.

Observed friction: a disconnected or unused cable slips behind a desk; cables cross, snag or hang into the workspace; cleaning and moving equipment becomes awkward; a person repeatedly retrieves and repositions the same lead.
  • Why organisers emerged: clips, channels and under-desk trays create a defined route and retention point. The workflow becomes “return cable to its slot” rather than “find the cable where it fell”.
  • What is solved: routing, visibility, retrieval and some snagging/clutter. Organisers are an established category, so ETHIC must not claim to have invented cable management.
  • Residual gap worth testing: a mount can still migrate or peel under cable pull. The C1 hypothesis is specifically force-induced mount movement—not “cables are messy”. It must be tested across surfaces, adhesive types, cable loads, temperature, removal and repeated cycles.
Product category reference: under-desk cable organiser example ↗. C1 remains a hypothesis; no prototype gate has cleared.
Micro case 03 · Finishing controlGreen / Caulking

The Caulking Finger

A finger is a ready-made shaping tool, but a bare finger can be sore after repeated work and makes a consistent bead profile depend on the person's motion and pressure.

Observed friction: smoothing caulk by hand can be uncomfortable over repeated jobs; the radius and pressure may vary; residue and surface contact complicate cleanup. The manufacturer describes the tool as designed and tested with professional painters, intended to smooth caulk and spare fingers from splinters and soreness.
  • What changed: a shaped, washable tool replaces direct skin contact as the working surface.
  • What it solves: a reusable surface profile, hand protection and repeatable contact geometry for a narrow task.
  • ETHIC lesson: it is an existing product precedent. The insight is useful for understanding behaviour engineering, but the product itself is not an unclaimed white space.
Product claims: The Caulking Finger product page ↗. Claims are attributed to the seller, not treated as independent test results.
Micro case 04 · Storage / handlingSilver / Fold-flat tools

Fold-flat measuring spoons

A set of separate spoons measures well, but several rigid pieces take drawer space and can scatter. A fold-flat form changes the stored geometry without changing the familiar measuring task.

Observed friction: small tools compete for drawer space, shift around, become separated or require handling one by one. A compact folding geometry aims to keep several measures in a single, flat object.
  • What changes: the storage footprint and the number of loose parts the user handles.
  • What it can solve: organisation and storage friction—not every measuring or cleaning problem.
  • ETHIC rule: an elegant example of physical redesign is still only a precedent. A new candidate needs an unmet residual need, usability evidence and prior-art review.
External design reference: Polygons product page ↗. The previously supplied video is not yet in the GitHub deployment, so it is intentionally not replaced by another clip.
F1–F4 takeaway: the product is not the starting point. Find the repeated task, name the hidden friction, map the workaround, then check whether existing products already solved it. The C1 cable-mount hypothesis stays in the candidate pipeline because its possible residual failure is narrower than generic cable organisation.
02 / F5 Fusion

NEO 1.
When a tool joins the human task instead of interrupting it.

F5 is the human–technology fusion category. The friction is not simply “people forget things”. It is the trade-off between staying present in a conversation and capturing what matters well enough to recall it and act later.

F5 · Human task × technology

Capture once. Recover the useful detail later.

Observed friction: during a meeting, interview, class or planning conversation, people choose between listening and writing notes. A phone recorder may capture audio but often leaves the user to start the recording, find the right file, replay it, summarise it, extract tasks and remember the follow-up.

Why NEO 1 matters: the official product description positions it as a wearable AI notetaker for offline and online conversations. It combines a discreet wearable, conversation capture, transcription/speaker recognition, summaries, tasks and reminders in a connected app. That is a fusion of physical form, sensors, AI processing and a later-retrieval workflow—not just a new voice recorder.

Human taskStay present in conversation
Technology layerWearable capture + AI structure
Desired outcomeRecall, tasks and follow-up

What friction it aims to reduce: divided attention, manual note preparation, lost commitments and the effort of searching a long recording for one decision.

What remains unproven for a given user: transcription accuracy, speaker attribution, battery endurance, consent/recording norms, privacy controls, connectivity and whether retrieval is more reliable than their existing phone/app workflow. Always-on capture has meaningful privacy implications.

MANRA reading: fusion is valuable when it removes a step from a habitual workflow. It is not automatically superior because it adds AI. Test the end-to-end job and the trade-offs.

F5 is the bridge: F1–F4 often reshape a physical object or step. F5 fuses a physical interface with a digital capability so a task can happen with less interruption. The same discovery discipline still applies: existing alternatives, user consent, reliability and residual gap must be verified.
03 / F6–F11

Technology friction.
Power is not enough; fit the tool to the moment.

F6–F11 cover frictions that arise around access, reach, technique, multi-step routines, maintenance and digital feedback. A portable vacuum/blower and the Dyson CameraJet are studied as real product examples across this field—not as products ETHIC invented or has independently validated.

F6 · Reach / setup / task fit

Cordless mini vacuum + blower

Why the category exists alongside large wired vacuums: a full-size vacuum can provide stronger deep-cleaning capacity, but it takes storage, a power source, setup and larger nozzles. It may be awkward for a few crumbs in a car seat seam, dust in a keyboard, a kitchen corner, a window track or a tight gap in a child's bedroom.

Friction observed: the user delays a small clean because the correct tool is inconvenient to retrieve, plug in, manoeuvre and put away. In other cases, the dirt is reachable only with a narrow nozzle or air blast.

What a compact cordless vacuum/blower changes: a grab-and-go body with a crevice tool can bring suction to narrow locations; a blower mode can dislodge dry dust from gaps; the same unit can cover multiple short tasks. The official Fanttik example combines vacuum, blower, inflation/deflation options and attachments.

Trade-off: compactness typically means a small dust bin, battery/runtime limits, filter maintenance and less suitability for whole-home deep cleaning. Do not compare “portable convenience” with “full-size capacity” as if they are the same job.

  • Use contexts: car seats and console gaps, office desks/keyboards, crumbs near kitchen edges, furniture seams and tight household corners.
  • Validation questions: Is setup time actually reduced? Can the nozzle reach the target? Does suction collect rather than scatter debris? How quickly do bin/filter maintenance and battery charging erase the convenience?

F7 · Technique / consistency

Manual vs powered brushing

Manual brushes can be effective, but outcomes depend on movement, duration, reach and the user's ability to maintain technique. Powered motion can assist some users; it does not make technique or coverage irrelevant.

Friction it addresses: the physical effort and consistency needed to repeat the right motion, particularly for users with dexterity limitations.

Evidence guardrail: the ADA says both manual and powered toothbrushes can be effective; powered products may be easier for some people with dexterity problems, older adults, children or dental appliances. The label “electric” alone proves nothing.

F8 · Coverage feedback

Knowing whether the routine was complete

A brush can be switched on while a user still misses surfaces or ends too early. Timers, motion guidance and coverage feedback attempt to make an otherwise invisible behaviour easier to see and repeat.

Friction it addresses: the gap between “I brushed” and “I followed a sufficiently complete, repeatable routine”.

Do not promise better clinical outcomes without product-specific evidence. Treat usability, adherence and independently measured cleaning efficacy as separate KPIs.

F9 · Blind-zone identification

Finding interdental gaps

Between-tooth spaces are difficult for users to see while brushing. Dyson CameraJet combines a macro camera and a targeted mouthrinse jet intended to identify and direct attention to those gaps during brushing.

Friction it targets: the user cannot easily see which small areas were missed, and flossing is often treated as a separate, easy-to-skip step.

This is the product's design thesis, not proof that all users clean better. Read the manufacturer description and independent first-look reporting together.

F10 · Setup / maintenance

Convenience has a maintenance cost

Portable vacuums need recharging, bin emptying and filter care. Smart oral-care systems can add reservoirs, charging docks, app setup and replaceable brush heads. Every new capability can introduce a new recurring burden.

Friction to measure: minutes of setup and upkeep per week, emptying frequency, failed starts, charging friction and how often maintenance is postponed.

ETHIC should count maintenance as part of the task—not treat it as an afterthought.

F11 · Recall / data hand-off

From device output to useful action

A device can capture data and still leave the person to search, interpret, transfer, verify or re-enter it. A good system makes the next action obvious without trapping the user in another silo.

Friction to examine: forgotten decisions, repeated data entry, incompatible exports, unreliable reminders, permission barriers and unclear correction/recovery paths.

Privacy, consent and app dependency are not optional extras; they are part of whether the workflow is genuinely easier.

Cross-cutting · Workflow fusion

Fewer steps—or more system complexity?

A combined device may bring brush, targeted flossing/rinse and guidance into one routine. But it can also add cost, liquid refilling, cleaning, connectivity and repair dependencies.

Decision question: does the full journey become simpler and more reliable for the target user, after setup, maintenance and failure handling are included?

This is the balance MANRA should measure before ETHIC commits investment.

Technology case · CameraJet

A system built around a very specific blind spot

Dyson announced CameraJet on 1 September 2026. Its stated design combines a 100k-pixel macro camera, machine-learning gap detection, targeted mouthrinse jets, powered brushing and an app with guided cleaning/coverage insights. Dyson's stated friction is that brushing, flossing and mouthwash are separate steps and that people may miss interdental gaps or under-brush.

What makes it a useful ETHIC study: it does not merely add a motor. It targets a hidden part of the task, then combines detection, action and feedback inside a single routine.

Reliability must be part of the story. Dyson said on 30 September 2026 that one early production batch had a jet-tube process issue with potential seepage and that it isolated affected products. Consumer Reports also reported a problematic first-use experience in its September 2026 first look. An ambitious fusion can reduce steps but introduce new failure modes; the product must be evaluated as a whole system.

F6–F11 takeaway: a technology is not the solution by itself. Study the complete cycle: access → use → result → cleanup → maintenance → feedback → next action. The job is to reduce total friction, not move it from one step into another.
04 / MANRA

Strategic intelligence.
Reduce uncertainty before investment.

MANRA is the strategy, discovery, qualification and growth layer inside ETHIC. It turns the company ambition into a repeatable decision system: what to observe, what to verify, what to test, when to stop and when evidence justifies investment.

MANRA · Strategic Intelligence & Growth

Do not confuse a clever workaround with a product opportunity.

People improvise for many reasons: cost, availability, habit, urgency or because a commercial product already failed them. MANRA must discover which reason applies, identify the specific residual failure and check whether the gap is broad enough to support a scalable business.

Operating principle: evidence before enthusiasm; qualification before spending; repeatability before scale.

05 / Where we stopped

Candidate status.
Keep the gates honest.

The current portfolio records a leading hypothesis, not a cleared product. The status must stay visible while the research moves from a pattern to a repeatable, manufacturable intervention.

P1 · Not cleared

C1 — force-induced mount migration

A cable mount may shift or peel when cable pull transfers force into its attachment. The broad cable-organiser category is established; this narrower mount-migration failure is the current test hypothesis, not a proven white space.

Current position: candidate only. No verified bench-test pass, proven global unmet need, patent clearance, production quote or paid conversion is claimed.

P2 · Investment gate unclaimed

P2 is the product-investment decision: it requires evidence of a real residual gap, a repeatable mechanism, plausible cost/margin, an addressable market and a viable manufacturing/compliance path.

Gate 1 · Evidence that the friction is real

Document the same failure across distinct users, contexts and installed products. Record surface, load, cable angle, temperature, cleaning, removal and repeated use.

Not yet passed
Gate 2 · Residual gap vs existing products

Compare current adhesive clips, screw-mounted holders, trays and routing systems. Identify where each succeeds, fails or is unsuitable. Search prior art before claiming novelty.

Not yet passed
Gate 3 · Physical proof

Prototype the minimum retention mechanism; test install time, pull/shear loads, repeatability, surface compatibility, removal damage and ageing.

Pending prototype
Gate 4 · User / investment proof

Observe real use, re-use, willingness to pay and purchase intent. Establish an achievable landed cost and a credible margin, not a target price alone.

08 / Global ambition

Build a global company—not just a successful demo.

The ambition is a substantial multinational enterprise: ETHIC identifies under-recognised friction, re-engineers useful interventions and builds repeatable product systems that can travel across countries. Global scale is the objective, not a present achievement or a guaranteed outcome.
