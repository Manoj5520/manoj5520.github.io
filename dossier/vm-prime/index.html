ACT 03 · BEHAVIOR ENGINEERING

“What is the smallest useful change?”

Start with behaviour and context, then choose the form. If a simple physical change solves it, do not add electronics. If technology adds a genuinely useful capability, investigate the wider workflow.

The first product must earn its way through evidence gates.
MANRA's discovery rule: “Search for forces, not just annoyances.” A visible nuisance may be the symptom. The research question is what repeatedly causes it, what people do to cope, and what is still not adequately solved.
02A / The ETHIC design thesis

From a small repeated action to a product that travels.

The visual sequence is a target operating path—not a claim that ETHIC has already validated or globally scaled a product. Scroll through the three layers: what happens, what changes, and what must be proven before scale.

Think of friction as the force a person repeatedly has to overcome. It might be physical, like a cable slipping; behavioural, like trying to remember a conversation; or systemic, like a cleaning task that requires multiple tools. The intervention should fit the cause, not the trend.

Layer 01 · Observe

Find the repeated force.

Who is doing what, where and how often? What do they hold, clip, wipe, search, lift or repeat to get the task done?

Output: a specific behaviour and context—not a product idea yet.
Layer 02 · Engineer

Make the smallest useful change.

Compare what exists. Fix the friction at the right layer: geometry, grip, storage, a new interface, a connected device or a better handoff.

Output: an intervention that can be compared against the workaround.
Layer 03 · Prove & scale

Make usefulness repeatable.

Check reliability, willingness to pay, manufacturing, service, safety, distribution and local context. A successful prototype is a beginning, not proof of global fit.

Output: proceed, narrow or stop—with the evidence recorded.

03 / Product evolution

Tooth powder → toothpaste format → powered brushing → connected oral care.

This is a useful example of how a familiar job can be served through changing product forms and technologies. It is not proof that one specific friction caused every change, or that older formats disappeared everywhere.

Before mass-market branded formats

People already had tools and routines.

Oral hygiene was not waiting for one invention. Ancient communities used chew-sticks—twigs with frayed ends—while dentifrices varied by place and time. The Smithsonian documents commercial and homemade powders using ingredients such as chalk/calcium carbonate, powdered cuttlebone, baking soda and charcoal. Some historical products used harsh abrasives or made claims that were not proven.

  • Chew-sticks and other simple cleaning implements.
  • Powders prepared at home or by druggists.
  • Rags/finger-applied cleaning and other local practices, which varied by culture and period.

Sources: Library of Congress · toothbrush history · Smithsonian · oral-care history.

What became a scalable format?

Not just the substance—the whole routine.

A jar or tube changes the package, but actual adoption also depends on formulation, preservation, dispensing, portability, cost, branding, access to shops, trust and advice from dental professionals. The collapsible tube enabled a different storage-and-dispensing routine; historical evidence does not identify one universally decisive friction.

Smithsonian’s U.S. context notes that toothpaste had become the more popular dentifrice by 1949, reaching roughly 75% of that market. That is a historical U.S. figure—not global market share. Tooth powder did not immediately disappear, and alternative formats still exist today.

Timeline sources: Colgate-Palmolive history · Smithsonian oral care.

Earlier format

Powder in a container

Powders were used to clean teeth. Users still had to take the powder from its container and apply it to a brush or other cleaning tool. Exact routines varied by period and person.

Historical reference: Smithsonian National Museum of American History.

→
Product-format change

Paste in a jar, then a tube

The Smithsonian records that Colgate marketed toothpaste in jars in the 1870s and introduced collapsible tubes in the 1890s. Tooth powder continued to be sold well into the twentieth century; the tube did not immediately erase every alternative.

History and object context: Smithsonian collection record.

Manual vs powered brushing: a capability gain, not a universal replacement.

A 2014 Cochrane review found that powered toothbrushes reduced plaque and gingivitis more than manual brushing on average in the included studies; the authors also noted that the clinical importance of the longer-term effects was unclear. Manual brushes remain useful, and a powered device adds cost, charging, replacement and maintenance. For ETHIC’s research, the question is which user or task benefits from the extra capability—and whether it improves routine outcomes enough to justify the complexity. Cochrane review ↗

1938 · Nylon bristlesNylon made the modern toothbrush bristle possible at commercial scale.
1954 · Powered brush milestonePhilippe-Guy Woog’s Broxodent was developed in Switzerland; powered brushes reached the U.S. market around 1960. Early models were positioned for some users with limited dexterity or orthodontic appliances.

Important accuracy note

What can we say about the friction?

It is reasonable to investigate handling, dosing, storage and mess as possible friction points for some users. But the cited sources do not establish one universal friction as the cause of the powder-to-paste transition. Nor do they establish that powered brushes were invented to compensate for soft modern diets or to simulate gum massage. Treat both as hypotheses unless credible historical or clinical sources support them.

The lesson for ETHIC is to work backward from the evidence: what job did a format change make easier, for whom, and what evidence links that change to adoption? Do not rewrite history to fit a business idea.

History sources: Library of Congress: toothbrush history · Smithsonian: nylon toothbrush · Smithsonian: oral-care collection.

Design reading · hypothesis, not historical certainty

What friction might the format change have addressed?

A powder container can require opening, retrieving an amount, and managing loose material. A paste in a jar changes the form; a collapsible tube gives users a portable container that can be closed and squeezed to dispense a portion.

The useful global-scale lesson

We cannot prove one single motive caused the tube to win. Product chemistry, oral-health claims, packaging, hygiene, brand trust, distribution, price and habit all mattered. Smithsonian material notes that paste had overtaken powder as the more popular form of dentifrice in the U.S. by 1949, at roughly 75% of that market.

For ETHIC, the lesson is to identify a repeated job, engineer a format that fits it, then validate adoption and distribution. Tube toothpaste is now a globally familiar format, but powders, tablets and other formats still exist; global scale is not the same as a universal solution.

Sources: Smithsonian Oral Care · Colgate corporate history.

04 / The discovery method

Start from a task. Find the workaround. Test the remaining gap.

Friction means a recurring point where a person's intended task meets resistance: extra effort, delay, uncertainty, repeated action, failure or compromise.

01 / OBSERVE

What is the user trying to do?

Describe the user, task, setting and moment. Avoid vague statements such as “cables are annoying”.

02 / EXPLAIN

What is causing the friction?

Look at force, fit, context, access, information, timing and repeated behaviour. A symptom is not yet a cause.

03 / COMPARE

What do people use now?

Inspect products and workarounds fairly. A cheap clip, tape, cloth, app or existing habit may already be good enough.

04 / QUALIFY

What remains unsolved?

Show what the current solution still makes the user do. Choose proceed, narrow or stop based on evidence.

The central question: WHAT DOES THE CURRENT SOLUTION STILL MAKE THE USER DO?
05 / Micro-frictions

F1–F4: small, everyday problems that repeat.

These are problem types, not product names. The same product can reduce more than one type of friction; the actual track depends on what happens in the real situation.

LANE A · F1–F4SMALL TASK · VISIBLE WORKAROUND · TESTABLE CONSEQUENCE
F1Fit & Hold

“Will it stay where I put it?”

What slips, shifts or detaches under force?

A desk cable organiser can keep loose cable ends close to the edge instead of letting them drop behind a desk. It is an established product category sold by many makers, not an undiscovered solution.

Research question: in a specific setup, does the mount itself move when the cable is pulled? What surface, load direction and attachment method are involved?

Existing-solution reference: UPLIFT cable clips.

F2Position & Access

“Why do I keep moving it?”

What must the user repeatedly hold, reposition or arrange?

Example: a craftsperson, cook or technician needs to put a tool down briefly, but the workspace has no suitable resting spot. The person keeps moving it out of the way and picking it up again.

Research question: how often does it happen, and does it cause delay, damage, contamination or another measurable consequence?

F3Format & Dispense

“Can I carry it, portion it and apply it cleanly?”

Does the container support the actual task and routine?

Powder formats can require opening a container and managing loose material. A collapsible tube supports closed storage and squeeze-based dispensing. This is a plausible usability comparison—not proof that one friction alone caused toothpaste tubes to spread.

Research question: measure spills, dosing control, portability, sealing, residue, cost and repeat use. Historical adoption also depended on formulation, branding, distribution and oral-health messaging.

Longer context: Toothpaste format case ↗.

F4Contact & Control

“What if the hand does not need to be the tool?”

Where does direct hand contact create mess, discomfort or inconsistent finishing?

The Caulking Finger is an existing purpose-shaped tool for smoothing and finishing a bead of caulk. It gives the task a dedicated contact surface instead of relying only on a bare fingertip, while preserving a simple, direct interaction.

Research lesson: a narrowly scoped micro-tool can address a small but practical task. The product proves an existing solution, not that nobody noticed the friction or that no alternatives exist.

06 / The bridge track

F5: a human task meets a technological capability.

F5 is not just “a gadget” and it is not an assumption that a current product is defective. Start with a real human difficulty, study what the technology does, and investigate the work that may remain across the wider workflow.

F5 · Human friction + technology

NEO 1: make room to listen; investigate the rest of the workflow.

1
The human task

During a meeting, class, site visit or customer conversation, a person tries to listen while remembering details, decisions and commitments.

2
The existing technology response

NeoSapien markets NEO 1 as a wearable AI notetaker for online and offline conversations that produces searchable transcripts and memories, summaries, extracted commitments and follow-ups through its app. The maker says source audio is processed and deleted while transcripts can remain in the service; consent, retention and recording rules must be considered before use.

3
What MANRA would investigate—not assume

Does the user still need to check a name or number, find a specific detail, get consent, confirm the owner/date of an action, or actually complete the follow-up?

4
The possible residual gap

Only a repeated, important remaining task supported by user evidence could justify another intervention. If NEO 1 plus the current workflow already works well, there may be no worthwhile gap for ETHIC to pursue.

Manufacturer specifications and promotional claims must be tested in context. This page does not claim NEO 1 has a defect. Conversation capture also raises consent, privacy and accuracy questions that must be handled responsibly.

07 / Technology-led tracks

F6–F11: investigate the wider product or system.

These examples move from one tiny action to a larger product workflow. Two reference products make the idea concrete: a cordless vacuum used across different places, and a connected oral-care device that combines brushing, camera-assisted targeting, liquid jetting and app guidance.

Reference A · Multi-task cordless cleaning

Fanttik Slim V10 APEX · 4-in-1 portable vacuum

One rechargeable, cordless handheld platform designed around multiple outputs: vacuuming, blowing, inflating and deflating. It is a useful example of packaging several recurring tasks into one portable tool—without claiming one mini device replaces every full-size cleaner.

19,000 Pa claimed max suction*15–30 min claimed runtime*USB-C · 2.5 h charge*Vacuum + blower + inflator + deflator*
  • What it helps solve: portable access to crumbs, dust and debris around car-seat seams, consoles, desks, keyboards and other awkward surfaces—with attachments that change reach or airflow direction.
  • Why the combination matters: one rechargeable device can switch jobs rather than requiring separate equipment for every small task. That is a concrete example of multiple capabilities being integrated into a portable system.
  • What remains: a small dust capacity, finite runtime, nozzle changes, filter cleaning, noise, charging and the risk of blowing debris into another corner. In cafés or larger vehicles, test hygiene, dust capture, bin capacity, duty cycle and operator workflow; do not assume a mini car vacuum is commercial-grade.
*Manufacturer-published figures, not independent performance tests: 19,000 Pa maximum suction, 15 minutes high / up to 30 minutes low, 120 ml dustbin, approximately 1.2 lb, USB-C charging and roughly 2.5-hour charging time. Reference B · Guided precision

Dyson CameraJet electric toothbrush

A higher-complexity oral-care system designed to combine three repeated steps—brushing, interdental cleaning and rinsing—into one guided routine. It integrates a macro camera, machine-learning gap targeting, a precision mouthrinse jet, variable sonic brushing and a connected app. That is a product-system redesign; it is not, by itself, proof of better outcomes for every user.

100k-pixel macro camera*Gap Optical Targeting™*12.5 ml anti-gravity tank*Variable sonic · anti-stall*App coverage map*Three brushing intensities*
  • Interdental gap targeting: Dyson describes a 100k-pixel macro camera and its Gap Optical Targeting™ system, which detects, tracks and predicts gaps between teeth before triggering a targeted mouthrinse jet. This targets a known routine challenge: brushing alone does not mechanically clean every interdental space.
  • Jet and fluid system: a conical precision jet and a 12.5 ml anti-gravity mouthrinse tank are designed to dispense controlled bursts while the brush changes angle. The refill/charging dock is designed to refill the tank at the touch of a button in about three seconds.
  • Brushing performance: a contoured dual-bristle head is designed to clean the gumline and lift surface stains; variable sonic oscillation is intended to reduce bristle stalling under contact. These are engineering claims that need independent assessment in the real world.
  • Feedback and personalisation: the MyDyson app offers live camera view, guided cleaning, coverage mapping and personalised technique feedback. The key behavioural question is whether users understand and repeat the improved technique after novelty wears off.
  • Privacy: Dyson says the camera is active during live viewing or auto-jetting and that images are not recorded or stored on the product or in the cloud. This is a manufacturer statement and should be checked against the current product documentation and app behaviour.
  • What to verify: targeting accuracy, clinical outcomes, usability, cleaning and maintenance, liquid leakage, robustness, brush-head cost, accessibility and whether the combined routine earns its added cost and complexity.

One taxonomy. Eleven research questions. No assumed winners.

12 / Three different concepts

Do not confuse problem types with a product-design ambition.

Two tracks use ideas that sound similar, and a third “fusion” is a separate design thesis. Their questions are different.

F5 · Problem type

Human task + technology

A person has a friction; an existing technology responds. Study the whole workflow and the work the person still does. NEO 1 is one reference case, not an assumed failed product.

F11 · Problem type

Tool-to-tool handoff

Two apps, devices or physical/digital tools do not transfer information or actions smoothly. Example to investigate: a user manually copies a commitment from chat to calendar and task list.

ETHIC · Design thesis

Simplicity + capability transformation

A future product may combine the clarity and manufacturability of a simple tool with a meaningful new ability. This is not the F5 track and not the F11 interoperability problem.

13 / What ETHIC is aiming for

A repeatable way to find, prove and scale useful interventions.

The ambition is not to be the most futuristic company. It is to identify problems that recur, matter to real people and remain poorly served—then build the smallest effective intervention and prove it across real contexts.

The intended differentiator

Method before machinery.

ETHIC’s working thesis is to begin with human behaviour and a specific task—not a gadget looking for a use case. MANRA investigates the workaround, compares existing solutions and identifies a possible residual gap. ETHIC then chooses whether a physical, digital or hybrid intervention is worth testing.

  • Observe: identify a repeated behaviour in a real setting.
  • Explain: separate the visible symptom from the likely cause.
  • Compare: acknowledge what current products already solve.
  • Test: prove the residual gap, reliability and willingness to pay.
  • Decide: proceed, narrow or stop before spending on scale.
Honest boundary: this is ETHIC’s intended operating model, not a proven competitive advantage yet. Differentiation exists only if the method consistently produces a result that customers value.

Where global scale could come from

Scale is a sequence of proofs—not a tagline.

1 · A repeatable need

Show the same task and residual problem in more than one user group or setting. A large-looking audience is not validated demand.

2 · A robust intervention

Test cost, materials, failure modes, safety, cleaning, maintenance and performance outside a single demonstration.

3 · A repeatable system

Prove purchasing behaviour, manufacturing quality, service, distribution and local requirements before claiming global readiness.

ETHIC’s intended difference: choose the simplest solution that adequately removes a real friction; add electronics, AI or connectivity only when the extra capability earns its complexity. The products shown are references, not ETHIC inventions.

14 / Where work stops today

The method is clearer. The product still has to earn its place.

The current achievement is a structured way to evaluate opportunities—not proof that a product has been validated, is patentable or is ready to scale.

STEP 01

Observe C1

Record a real cable-anchor failure in context. Count instances; distinguish what is seen from what is inferred.

STEP 02

Bench-test the cause

Test surface, attachment, pull direction, force and repeated cycles. Keep results and failures measurable.

STEP 03

Compare real options

Review at least three alternatives, identify what each already does well and state one residual gap.

STEP 04

Qualify, then decide

Check unmet need, ETHIC fit, broader potential, a clear intervention and prototype feasibility before spending further.
